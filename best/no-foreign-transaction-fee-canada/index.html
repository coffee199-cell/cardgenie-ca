Best No Foreign Transaction Fee Credit Cards in Canada

Most Canadian credit cards quietly charge a 2.5% foreign transaction fee on every purchase made in a non-Canadian currency — whether you're abroad or just shopping on a US website. A no-FX-fee card eliminates that surcharge entirely. For frequent travellers or anyone who shops online from US retailers, the savings can easily exceed a card's annual fee within a few months.

What the foreign transaction fee actually costs

Nearly every Canadian credit card adds 2.5 percent to purchases billed in a currency other than Canadian dollars. It applies to hotel bookings abroad, restaurant bills on holiday, and any online purchase from a retailer that bills in US dollars, which includes a large share of everyday online shopping.

The fee is charged on top of the exchange rate the payment network applies, so it is a genuine additional cost rather than part of the conversion. It also appears on your statement folded into the converted amount, which is why most people never notice paying it. On CA$5,000 of foreign spending in a year, the fee is CA$125.

Compare that to what a rewards card returns on the same spending. A card earning 2 percent back on CA$5,000 produces CA$100. The fee is larger than the reward, which means a high-earning card with a foreign transaction fee loses to a modest card without one on any spending billed in foreign currency.

Who actually needs one of these cards

The obvious case is frequent international travel. The less obvious and far more common case is online shopping. If you buy from US retailers, subscribe to services billed in US dollars, or use marketplaces that settle in a foreign currency, you are paying the fee every month without leaving home.

Add up your foreign currency spending for the past year before deciding. Below roughly CA$2,000 a year, the fee costs about CA$50 and rarely justifies switching cards or paying a higher annual fee. Above CA$5,000 a year, the fee alone can exceed a premium card's annual fee, and eliminating it becomes the single most valuable change you can make to your card setup.

The fastest way to decide

Estimate your annual foreign currency spending and multiply by 0.025. If that number is larger than the difference in annual fees between your current card and a no-FX card, switching pays for itself. If it is smaller, keep the card that earns more at home and accept the fee on occasional foreign purchases.

Two ways to solve the same problem

The first approach is a card that charges no foreign transaction fee at all. This is the cleanest solution because it applies to every foreign purchase automatically, with no category rules and nothing to remember. The Scotiabank Gold American Express is the notable example at a moderate fee, since it combines no FX fee with a strong grocery and dining rate, which is unusual at that price.

The second approach is a card that charges the fee but pays enough extra cash back on foreign currency purchases to more than cover it. Rogers Bank and Fido cards work this way, returning a higher rate on US dollar spending that exceeds the 2.5 percent surcharge. The result is similar, but it applies only to US dollars rather than all foreign currencies, so it does not help in Europe or Asia.

Which approach suits you depends on where you spend. Cross-border online shopping in US dollars is well served by the second approach. International travel to multiple countries is better served by the first.

Two things that undo the saving

Dynamic currency conversion is the first. When a foreign merchant or ATM offers to charge you in Canadian dollars instead of the local currency, the exchange rate applied is set by the merchant and is almost always worse than the network rate, often by 3 to 5 percent. Accepting that offer costs more than the fee you switched cards to avoid. Always choose to be billed in the local currency.

Cash advances are the second. Withdrawing cash abroad on a credit card starts interest immediately at the cash advance rate, typically above 22 percent, with no grace period and usually a flat fee on top. No foreign transaction fee benefit offsets that. Use a debit card for cash and keep the credit card for purchases.

Do not choose on the fee alone

A no-FX card still spends most of its life on domestic purchases. If it earns poorly at home, the fee saving on a two-week trip may not cover a year of weaker everyday rewards. The strongest choice is a card that earns well domestically and happens to waive the FX fee, rather than a weak card whose only feature is the waiver.

This is why the Scotiabank Gold American Express appears on the travel card rankings as well. Its head-to-head against the strongest competing grocery earner is covered in Amex Cobalt vs Scotiabank Gold, where the foreign transaction fee is one of the deciding factors. Other card comparisons work through the same trade-off for different pairs.

The Short Version

Multiply your annual foreign currency spending by 2.5 percent. If that figure is material, a no foreign transaction fee card will save you more than most rewards rates will earn you. Pick one that also earns well on domestic spending, always decline dynamic currency conversion, and never take a cash advance abroad.
