Best Travel Credit Cards in Canada

Canada's top travel cards go beyond basic points — the right one can offset your annual fee many times over through welcome bonuses, lounge access, and elevated earn rates on flights and hotels. We ranked these cards on total rewards value, redemption flexibility, and travel perks that matter most to Canadian cardholders.

How to choose a travel credit card

Travel cards are harder to compare than cash back cards because the reward is not denominated in dollars. A card advertising 5x points is not automatically better than one advertising 3x, because the two programs may value a point differently, and one of them may be a currency you will never redeem well.

Start by deciding which of three things you actually want. Some travellers want flexible points that convert to several airlines and hotels. Some want a single airline's currency because they fly that airline anyway. Some want travel benefits, meaning lounge access, checked bags and insurance, and treat the points as secondary. These are different products and comparing them by earn rate alone produces nonsense.

CardGenie values Aeroplan points at 1.5 cents and most flexible points programs at 1 cent when ranking cards. Those figures are deliberately conservative. A well-timed transfer to an airline partner can beat them substantially, but only for people who will do the work. If you would redeem points as a statement credit against a travel purchase, the conservative valuation is the honest one.

The annual fee is the whole question

Travel cards carry the highest annual fees in the Canadian market, from about CA$120 to CA$799. A high fee is not automatically bad, but it has to be recovered by something concrete. Work out the recovery before applying: an annual travel credit, a companion fare, lounge visits you would otherwise pay for, or checked bags you actually check.

Benefits you will not use are worth zero regardless of their stated value. A CA$799 card advertising lounge access is a poor purchase for someone who flies twice a year from an airport where the lounge is closed at their departure time. Count only the benefits that survive contact with your real travel pattern, then compare the remainder to the fee.

The fastest way to decide

If you fly one airline several times a year, take that airline's co-branded card and use the card-linked benefits. If you fly whoever is cheapest, take a flexible points card or a straightforward cash back card, because airline currency you cannot use is worth nothing. If you travel internationally more than occasionally, prioritise a card with no foreign transaction fee before you compare earn rates at all.

Foreign transaction fees quietly outrank earn rates

The standard Canadian foreign transaction fee is 2.5 percent, charged on any purchase billed in a currency other than Canadian dollars. On a two-week trip with CA$3,000 of foreign spending, that fee costs CA$75. A card earning 2x points on travel returns roughly CA$60 of value on the same spending at a 1 cent valuation. The fee is larger than the reward.

This is why a no foreign transaction fee card frequently beats a higher-earning card for anyone who travels. It is also why the Scotiabank Gold American Express appears on both this list and the FX-free list: it combines a strong grocery and dining rate at home with no surcharge abroad. The comparison against its closest competitor is covered in Amex Cobalt vs Scotiabank Gold, and the full FX-free shortlist is under no foreign transaction fee credit cards.

Travel insurance is the underrated benefit

Premium travel cards bundle emergency medical coverage, trip cancellation, trip interruption, flight delay, lost baggage and rental car collision damage. Bought separately, comparable coverage for a family can cost more than the annual fee on its own, which makes insurance the most reliable way a travel card justifies itself.

Read the eligibility rules before relying on it. Emergency medical coverage usually has an age limit, often dropping sharply past 65, and typically caps the number of consecutive days covered per trip. Most policies require the trip to be charged to the card, sometimes in full. Coverage that fails on a technicality at the moment you need it is worse than no coverage, because you did not buy a backup.

Aeroplan cards are a separate decision

Aeroplan is the dominant airline currency in Canada, and Air Canada co-branded cards are the most common travel cards Canadians hold. They earn fewer points per dollar than the best everyday earners but attach benefits to the card itself, most notably a free first checked bag for the cardholder and companions on the same booking.

Whether that trade is worth it depends entirely on how often you check a bag on Air Canada. The full working is in Amex Cobalt vs TD Aeroplan Visa Infinite, and if you have already settled on Aeroplan, the Aeroplan card rankings compare the issuers against each other.

The Short Version

Decide whether you want flexible points, one airline's currency, or benefits. Check the foreign transaction fee before the earn rate, because on international spending the fee is usually larger than the reward. Count only the benefits you will genuinely use against the annual fee. Then run your own spending through the CardGenie calculator, which applies each card's caps and subtracts its fee rather than ranking on headline rates.
