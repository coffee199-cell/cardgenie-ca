Best Cash Back Credit Cards in Canada

Cash back cards are the simplest reward in personal finance: spend money, get a percentage back, no points conversion required. The right card depends on where you spend the most — the best grocery earn rate does nothing for you if your biggest monthly expense is gas. We ranked these six cards on net annual value for a typical Canadian household.

How to choose a cash back card

Cash back is the easiest reward to evaluate because a dollar back is worth a dollar. That removes the guesswork that makes points cards hard to compare, but it does not make the choice simple. Three things decide which cash back card actually pays you the most, and only one of them appears in the advertising.

The first is category fit. A card paying 4 percent on groceries and 1 percent on everything else beats a flat 2 percent card only if groceries are a large share of your spending. Work out what you spend monthly in each category before comparing rates, because the ranking changes completely between a household that cooks at home and one that eats out.

The second is the category cap, and it is the reason most people earn less than they calculated. Every premium cash back card in Canada limits its top rate to a set amount of annual or monthly spending. Above that ceiling the card pays 1 percent, the same as a free card. Caps are frequently shared across several categories, so spending on gas can quietly consume the allowance you assumed was reserved for groceries.

The third is the annual fee, which is a fixed cost against a proportional reward. A CA$120 fee needs roughly CA$4,000 of grocery spending a year at a 3 percent premium over a free card just to break even. Below that level, a no annual fee card wins even though its rate is lower.

Why the caps matter more than the rates

Consider two cards a household might reasonably compare. The Scotiabank Momentum Visa Infinite pays 4 percent on groceries and recurring bills, sharing a CA$25,000 annual allowance between them. The TD Cash Back Visa Infinite pays 3 percent on groceries, gas and recurring bills, sharing a single CA$15,000 annual allowance across all three.

On a household spending CA$800 a month on groceries, CA$200 on gas and CA$300 on recurring bills, the Momentum card stays under its cap all year and nets about CA$576 after its CA$120 fee. The TD card reaches its CA$15,000 ceiling before the year is out and nets about CA$437 after its CA$139 fee. The gap is roughly CA$139, and almost none of it comes from the difference between 4 percent and 3 percent. It comes from the cap.

Reverse the spending toward fuel and the answer flips, because TD pays 3 percent on gas where Momentum pays 2 percent. The full working for both profiles is in the Scotiabank Momentum vs TD Cash Back comparison.

The fastest way to decide

Add up your annual spending in the categories a card pays its top rate on. If that total is below the card's cap, compare rates. If it is above the cap, the effective rate is lower than advertised, and a card with a smaller headline rate but a larger allowance often pays more. Then subtract the annual fee from both and compare what is left.

When a no annual fee card is the better answer

Premium cash back cards make sense at moderate to high spending. Below roughly CA$1,500 a month of card spending, the fee usually eats the advantage. A no annual fee card paying 2 percent on a few chosen categories can beat a CA$120 card paying 4 percent, simply because there is no fee to recover.

No annual fee cards are also the right choice as a second card. If your main card caps out in the autumn, moving the overflow spending to a free card recovers the difference between 1 percent and whatever the free card pays. See the best no annual fee credit cards in Canada for that shortlist.

The same logic applies to income eligibility. Both premium Visa Infinite cash back cards publish a CA$60,000 personal or CA$100,000 household minimum income. Applicants below that threshold are not choosing between these cards at all, and a free card is the practical starting point.

Check how the cash back is paid out

Not all cash back reaches you the same way. Some issuers credit your account once a year on a set statement, which means leaving the card mid-year can forfeit rewards you have already earned. Others let you redeem any time against a statement balance, sometimes with a minimum. A card that pays out annually is worth slightly less than an identical card that pays continuously, and the difference matters if you rotate cards.

Check the merchant coding too. Grocery multipliers apply to purchases the payment network classifies as grocery stores. Warehouse clubs, general merchandise retailers and delivery platforms often code differently, so the 4 percent you expected at a big-box store may arrive as 1 percent. If most of your food shopping happens somewhere that codes as general merchandise, the category rate is worth less than it appears.

Cash back on foreign currency spending

Most Canadian cash back cards charge a 2.5 percent foreign transaction fee, which erases the reward on anything bought in another currency. A card paying 2 percent back and charging 2.5 percent in fees is a net loss on US dollar purchases. If a meaningful part of your spending is in foreign currencies, that fee outweighs the earn rate, and the relevant list is the no foreign transaction fee cards instead.

The Short Version

Match the card's top category to where your money actually goes, confirm your annual spending in that category fits under the cap, and subtract the fee. If you spend heavily on groceries and bills, a premium card with a large allowance wins. If you spend moderately, a no annual fee card usually wins. Compare the two finalists directly rather than reading rankings, and run your own numbers through the CardGenie calculator before applying.
